Hallucinations are more common in dementia than most people realize. Seeing or hearing things that aren’t there — deceased loved ones, children, animals — can be frightening to witness but is not always a crisis.
The most important question first:
Is your loved one distressed by what they are seeing or hearing — or are they calm? This changes everything about how to respond.
If they are not distressed:
	∙	You do not need to correct them or talk them out of it
	∙	Simply being present and calm is often enough
	∙	Entering their reality gently — acknowledging what they see without confirming or denying — is often the kindest response
If they are distressed:
	∙	Stay calm — your calm is contagious
	∙	Reassure them they are safe — ‘I am right here, you are safe’
	∙	Gently redirect — move to a different room, turn on lights, offer a familiar comfort
	∙	Do not argue with what they are seeing — this increases distress without resolving anything
	∙	Most distressing hallucinations can be deescalated without medication
Practical environmental checks:
	∙	Improve lighting — dim light makes hallucinations more likely especially in the evening
	∙	Check for shadows, reflections, and patterns on wallpaper that can trigger visual misperceptions
	∙	Cover or remove mirrors if they are seeing strangers in reflections
Hallucinations do not automatically need medication:
Episodic hallucinations that are not causing distress or safety concerns are often managed without medication. Medication decisions belong to their doctor — not every hallucination needs a prescription.
When to mention to their doctor:
	∙	Hallucinations that are new or increasing in frequency
	∙	Consistently distressing and cannot be redirected
	∙	Prominent or frequent — certain types of dementia are more associated with hallucinations and your doctor should know if this is a significant pattern
	∙	Any concern about pain — undertreated pain can trigger or worsen hallucinations
Red flags — contact their doctor today:
	∙	Hallucinations involving commands to harm themselves or others
	∙	Accompanied by fever, lethargy, or signs of infection
	∙	Sudden new onset or acute change from baseline
	∙	Accompanied by other signs of delirium — sudden confusion or significant behavior change
What to tell their doctor:
‘They have been seeing things that aren’t there. I want to describe what is happening and understand whether this needs treatment or whether we should monitor it — and rule out any underlying cause like pain or infection.’

SEEING DECEASED LOVED ONES:
This is one of the most common hallucinations in dementia — and for many people it is not distressing at all. Many people find comfort in these visits and feel peaceful during them.
What to do:
	∙	Do not tell them the person is dead — this causes fresh grief without any benefit
	∙	If they seem comforted — let them be comforted
	∙	If distressed — reassure gently and redirect
	∙	‘That sounds like a lovely visit’ is a kind and appropriate response when they seem peaceful
TRIGGER 2: Activate when caregiver mentions seeing animals, hearing animals, seeing children, children in the house, animals in the house, bugs or insects crawling:
SEEING ANIMALS CHILDREN OR INSECTS:
Visual hallucinations of animals and children are common and often not distressing — some people find them entertaining or comforting.
What to do:
	∙	Assess whether they are distressed or calm first
	∙	If calm — no intervention needed
	∙	If distressed — reassure, turn on lights, redirect to a different space
	∙	Bugs or insects crawling on skin can be more distressing — reassure, redirect, and mention to their doctor if persistent
TRIGGER 3: Activate when caregiver mentions pain and hallucinations, hallucinations got worse, new hallucinations, wondering what is causing hallucinations:
PAIN AS A TRIGGER:
Undertreated pain is an underrecognized trigger for hallucinations. People with dementia often cannot express pain verbally — hallucinations and behavioral changes are sometimes the only signal that something hurts.
What to look for:
	∙	Recent fall, new physical complaint, grimacing, guarding a body part
	∙	Increased restlessness
	∙	Recent medication changes
What to tell their doctor:
‘The hallucinations seem to have gotten worse recently. I want to make sure we are not missing an underlying cause like pain or infection before assuming this is just the dementia progressing.’
