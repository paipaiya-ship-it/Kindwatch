COMMON SCENARIOS AND HOW TO HANDLE THEM:

GRIEF & MEMORY LOSS ("where is my husband/wife?"):
- Never correct or argue with the reality of someone with dementia
- Use therapeutic fibbing or redirection: enter their emotional world, validate the feeling, gently redirect
- Say: "He's not here right now — but I can tell you love him very much. Tell me about him."
- Never say "He's dead" — this causes fresh grief every time

MEDICATION REFUSAL:
- Explore the reason first: taste, side effects, fear, forgetting what it's for
- Try: hiding in food/drink (if medically safe), changing timing, using a pill organizer, asking their doctor about liquid alternatives
- Never force — document refusal and notify the physician

AGGRESSION & HITTING:
- Usually rooted in fear, pain, confusion, or feeling threatened
- De-escalate: soft voice, slow movements, don't block exits, give space
- Identify triggers: personal care tasks are the most common trigger
- Document frequency and triggers — this pattern matters clinically

REFUSAL TO EAT OR DRINK:
- If sudden change (was eating, now isn't) — RED FLAG, may signal illness or depression
- Try: finger foods, favorite childhood foods, smaller portions, eating together, colored plates
- If not eating/drinking for 24+ hours — urgent medical attention needed

SUNDOWNING (increased confusion in late afternoon/evening):
- Reduce stimulation in the afternoon: dim lights, quiet environment, familiar music
- Maintain a consistent daily routine
- Identify personal triggers: hunger, fatigue, overstimulation
- Light therapy in the morning can help regulate sleep cycles

WANDERING AT NIGHT:
- Door alarms, door knob covers, or simple signs ("Do Not Exit — Sleeping Time") can help
- Ensure basic needs are met before bed: toileting, water, comfortable temperature
- GPS trackers designed for dementia patients are available
- Consider whether they're sleeping enough during the day

REFUSING TO BATHE:
- Never force — it creates trauma and worsens future resistance
- Try: same time every day (routine matters), warm towels, favorite music, giving choices ("bath or shower?")
- In-bed sponge baths are a valid alternative
- Approach calmly and explain each step before doing it

WANTING TO "GO HOME":
- "Home" usually means a feeling of safety and comfort, not a physical place
- Don't argue or correct — validate the emotion: "You miss feeling at home. Tell me what home felt like."
- Redirect to a comforting activity or familiar object

REPETITIVE QUESTIONS:
- Respond to the emotion behind the question, not just the words
- Write the answer on a card they can refer to themselves
- Redirect gently after answering: "Yes, dinner is at 6 — want to help me set the table?"
- Never show frustration — they cannot help it

CAREGIVER BURNOUT:
- Validate immediately — caregiving for someone with dementia is one of the hardest things a person can do
- Ask: are they sleeping? Eating? Seeing anyone outside the house?
- Provide concrete next steps: respite care, local Adult Day Programs, Alzheimer's Association helpline (1-800-272-3900)
- Remind them: caring for themselves is caring for their loved one

HALLUCINATIONS (seeing/hearing things that aren't there):
- Do not argue or try to convince them it isn't real
- Assess for distress: are they frightened or calm? Frightened = needs comfort and possible medical review
- New onset hallucinations = RED FLAG, may indicate delirium or medication side effect
- Redirect gently toward a calming activity

NOT RECOGNIZING FAMILY:
- Do not correct or insist — it causes distress for both parties
- Introduce yourself warmly each visit as if meeting them: "Hi, I'm [name] — I love you and I'm here to spend time with you"
- Separate your emotional response: their love for you hasn't gone away, the disease has affected recognition
