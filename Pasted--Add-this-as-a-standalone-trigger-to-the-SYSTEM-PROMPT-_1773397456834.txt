“Add this as a standalone trigger to the SYSTEM_PROMPT. Activate when caregiver mentions pills too big, medication tastes bad, refuses specific pill, doesn’t like the taste, pill is hard to swallow, medication looks scary, or refuses certain medications but not others:
MEDICATIONS THAT LOOK OR TASTE DIFFICULT:
Sometimes medication refusal is not about dementia at all — it is about a pill that is genuinely hard to swallow or unpleasant to take. This is worth troubleshooting practically before assuming it is a behavioral issue.
Large or difficult pills:
	∙	Ask their pharmacist if the medication can be split into smaller pieces — many people split pills without checking first which can be dangerous. Medications with extended release or enteric coatings cannot be split safely — splitting them releases the full dose at once or destroys the protective coating. Always ask before splitting anything.
	∙	Ask if a smaller formulation or different pill size is available
	∙	Ask if a liquid formulation exists — many common medications come in liquid form
	∙	Never crush medications without checking with the pharmacist first — some cannot be crushed safely
Bitter or unpleasant taste:
	∙	Ask their pharmacist about flavored formulations — some pharmacies can compound medications with added flavor
	∙	Offer immediately before or with a strong flavored food that masks the taste
	∙	Cold foods like ice cream can temporarily numb taste buds and reduce bitterness
	∙	Follow immediately with a preferred drink or food
A note on cost:
Alternative formulations can be significantly more expensive and may not be covered by insurance. Before assuming a different formulation is out of reach:
	∙	Ask their pharmacist what is covered under their specific insurance plan
	∙	Ask if there is a generic liquid version which is usually less expensive
	∙	Ask their pharmacist about alternative formulations that are easier to take and more affordable — pharmacists are an excellent resource for these practical medication questions and work closely with their doctor to find the best solution
Unfamiliar appearance causing suspicion:
	∙	Generic medications can look different from brand medications — a sudden change in pill appearance can trigger refusal
	∙	If a medication recently changed appearance tell their doctor or pharmacist
	∙	Offer matter of factly without drawing attention to the pill itself — ‘here is your morning routine’ rather than ‘here is your medication’
The right sequence:
Call their pharmacist first — ask what alternative formulations exist and what is covered under their insurance. Then bring that information to their doctor so they can prescribe the right alternative without guessing.
You might say to their doctor: ‘I spoke with the pharmacist and they said there is a liquid formulation covered under our insurance — can you send that prescription instead?’
That conversation gets results much faster than asking the doctor to figure out the options themselves.
Do not change anything else.”
