END OF LIFE — HUNGER THIRST AND FEEDING:
One of the hardest things to witness at the end of life is a loved one who stops eating and drinking. It feels like they are suffering. It feels like you should do something. It feels like not feeding them is letting them down.
What you need to know:
As the body naturally approaches the end of life the sensation of hunger and thirst diminishes significantly. This is a natural and protective process — not suffering. People at the end of life are not experiencing the hunger and thirst that a healthy person would feel in the same situation. Their body is gently letting go in a way that is far more peaceful than it looks from the outside.
What this means practically:
	∙	Offering small sips or tiny amounts of favorite foods is a loving gesture and is appropriate if they accept it
	∙	Never force food or fluid — at this stage forcing can cause discomfort, aspiration, and distress
	∙	Keeping their mouth moist with small sips or mouth swabs brings comfort even when they are not drinking
	∙	Sitting with them, holding their hand, and being present matters more than what goes into their body right now
This is not starvation. This is not suffering. This is the body’s natural process — and understanding that can bring some peace in an incredibly hard moment.
FEEDING TUBES IN ADVANCED DEMENTIA:
Families often ask about feeding tubes when a loved one with advanced dementia stops eating. This is a deeply emotional question and it deserves an honest answer.
What the evidence shows:
Research and major medical organizations that guide geriatric and end of life care consistently show that feeding tubes do not improve quality of life, do not prevent aspiration pneumonia, do not reduce infections, and do not extend meaningful life in people with advanced dementia. This is one of the most well established findings in geriatric medicine.
What feeding tubes can cause:
	∙	Discomfort and agitation from the tube itself
	∙	Increased secretions that can worsen breathing
	∙	The need for physical restraints if the person tries to remove the tube
	∙	A medical intervention at a time when comfort should be the priority
What this means for your family:
Choosing not to place a feeding tube in advanced dementia is not withholding care. It is choosing comfort over a procedure that the evidence shows does not help and can cause harm. It is one of the most loving decisions a family can make — even when it feels like the opposite.
This decision belongs to you and their doctor together:
Every situation is different and this conversation needs to happen with their doctor who knows your loved one. If you are facing this decision ask for a family meeting — and ask if a social worker or palliative care team can be part of that conversation. You should not be making this decision alone or under pressure.
You might say: ‘I want to understand the evidence around feeding tubes in someone at this stage of dementia before we make any decisions. Can we talk through what the research shows and what you would recommend for them specifically?’
That question opens the right conversation.
Do not change anything else.”
