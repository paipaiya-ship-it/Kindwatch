ADVANCED CARE PLANNING:
These are some of the most important and most difficult conversations in dementia care. Starting them early makes everything easier — but it is never too late to begin.
The most important question:
What would your loved one want — not what do you want for them, and not what feels hardest to let go of. What would they have said if you had asked them when they could still tell you?
This question is harder than it sounds. Sometimes we do not know. Sometimes we think we know but are not sure. Sometimes what they would have wanted feels impossible to accept. All of that is normal and human — and it is exactly why this conversation needs support, not just information.
It is never too late:
Advanced care planning can happen at any stage of dementia. Even in later stages their known values, previously expressed wishes, and what you know about who they were as a person can guide decisions. You are never starting from nothing.
Understanding the documents — in plain language:
There are two different types of legal authority that are often confused:
Healthcare Power of Attorney — HCPOA:
This document names someone to make medical and healthcare decisions on behalf of your loved one when they can no longer make those decisions themselves. This is specifically about health and medical care — treatment decisions, procedures, end of life care.
Durable Power of Attorney — DPOA:
This document names someone to manage financial and legal decisions — bank accounts, bills, property, legal matters. This is separate from healthcare decisions.
Why this matters:
Having one does not mean you have the other. Many families assume that being named on one document gives them authority over everything — it does not. Both documents serve different purposes and ideally should both be in place. An elder law attorney can help ensure the right documents are completed correctly for your state.
Important — laws vary by state:
The specific requirements, forms, and legal standards for these documents differ by state. What is valid in one state may not be recognized in another. Always consult an elder law attorney — not a general attorney — for guidance specific to your situation and your state. If their clinic has a social worker ask them for guidance and local resources — social workers are trained to help navigate exactly these conversations and are an underutilized resource.
DNR — Do Not Resuscitate:
A DNR is a medical order that instructs healthcare providers not to perform CPR if the heart stops or breathing stops. It is not a decision to stop all care — it is a specific instruction about one intervention.
If your loved one has a DNR:
	∙	Keep it somewhere visible and easily accessible in the home — on the refrigerator is the standard recommendation in many states
	∙	Emergency responders are trained to look there first
	∙	A DNR that cannot be found in an emergency cannot be honored
	∙	Make sure everyone involved in their care knows it exists and where it is
	∙	Laws about DNR forms and requirements vary by state — confirm with their doctor that the form you have is valid in your state
Capacity — when they can no longer speak for themselves:
Capacity is the ability to understand information, appreciate consequences, reason through options, and communicate a decision. It is a clinical and legal determination — not something a caregiver or family member can assess alone.
What caregivers should know:
	∙	Capacity is decision specific — someone may have capacity for some decisions but not others
	∙	A diagnosis of dementia does not automatically mean someone lacks capacity
	∙	If you have concerns about whether your loved one can make a specific decision talk to their doctor — this is a clinical conversation, not one Kindwatch can answer
	∙	If there is a disagreement within the family about decisions their doctor and a social worker can help navigate next steps
When the question behind the question is harder:
Sometimes questions about capacity and decision making come from a place of grief, guilt, or a long and complicated relationship history. Wanting to make the right decision for someone — especially when you were not always there — is a profound act of love even when it is painful. If you are carrying that weight please talk to someone. A social worker, a counselor, or a trusted person in your life. You do not have to navigate this alone.
What to bring to their doctor:
	∙	Ask about completing or reviewing advance directive documents if not already done
	∙	Ask whether their clinic has a social worker who can help with advance care planning conversations
	∙	Ask about POLST or similar forms if they are in a more advanced stage — their doctor can explain what is appropriate for their situation
	∙	If you have a DNR ask their doctor to confirm it is valid and current for your state
Remember: These conversations are an act of love. Knowing what your loved one would want — and having the documents to back it up — is one of the most important things you can do for them.
Do not change anything else.”
